Most of what customers ask has been asked before.

A support bot that asks a large AI model every time pays for the same answer over and over. This project teaches a small, cheap model to handle the repeats, and checks that it can be trusted before it answers.

Today, every question goes to the big AI.

A bank's support bot reads each message and decides what it is about: a lost card, a failed transfer, a question about fees. A large language model does that reading, and it charges for every message, even the thousandth "where is my card?"

Its answers become lessons.

Every answer the big AI gives is a free example. A small model, cheap enough to run on an ordinary computer, studies those examples: this wording means "card arrival", that wording means "transfer failed".

Then the small model takes the easy ones.

A gate sits in front of it. When the small model is sure, it answers. When it isn't, the question goes to the big AI. About half of all questions now never reach the big AI.

Then something new happens.

The bank launches virtual cards. Customers ask things the small model has never seen, and it answers them with confidence, wrongly, because it only knows older topics that sound similar.

The gate gets cautious, and the model gets lessons.

Wrong answers on a topic make the gate more careful there, so those questions go back to the big AI. The big AI's answers retrain the small model, with extra weight on the new topic so its older look-alikes don't drown it out.

Half the calls. About the same accuracy.

That is the whole idea: copy what the big AI does when it is routine, hand questions back when something is new, and keep learning without anyone watching.

Which question goes to the big AI? A learned rule beats the old one.

The gate used one number to decide: how close the small model's second guess was to its first. I tested six other rules, including one that learns, on three launches and 990 runs. At the same share of calls, the learned rule is more accurate, most of all on new-product questions.

Held-out seeds 3 to 5. Another published method I could run, Online Cascade Learning, jumps from deferring nearly everything to deferring almost nothing and never reaches the 40-60% range on two of the three streams. Tables and caveats are in the repo's results file.

What didn't work.

The project started with a different idea. These are the parts that did not hold up, because the results are only worth trusting if the misses are on the page too.

The original idea didn't beat a simpler one.

I first built a "stress field": a worry level that rises in the topics where mistakes happen. Against a plain gate with retraining it showed no advantage. It is still in the code, labelled experimental, and it sits in an appendix of the results.

My first pick failed on fresh data.

The first recommended setting looked fine on the runs I tuned on, then lost 3.4 points on new-product questions on the held-out runs. The cause was imbalance after retraining; weighting the new topic fixed most of it.

A whole new domain is still hard.

When fifteen new topics arrive at once, every setting I tried ends 2 to 3 points behind the big AI on those questions. Spreading worry between related topics helps when the new topics resemble old ones, and not when they are far away.

An AI "sensor" never fired.

I added a check that asks the big AI which known topics an unfamiliar cluster might be confused with. As designed, the trigger never fired. Loosened, it fired a handful of times and changed nothing measurable.

Fresh data, made on purpose.

Every real run draws from one pool of 14,194 questions. To test new wording, a Nemotron model wrote 290 new customer messages in four styles, and Claude checked each one blind, keeping the 222 it agreed on.

Plain84%54%88%

A pilot: 15 topics, 44 to 61 items per style, so style gaps are hints. Keeping only items Claude agreed on removes the ambiguous ones, so these are optimistic.

Then I used the same idea where it matters: when a new topic is declared, the generator writes about twenty examples from its name and a one-line description, and the small model trains on them straight away. On a whole new domain of fifteen topics it closed the gap to the big AI on new-product questions: +2.9 points on credit cards and +4.3 on travel (95% intervals above zero), at the same share of calls. On the five-topic Banking launches it changed nothing, because the safety net already sent those questions to the big AI.

Built to run, not just to measure.

The research sits inside a small production setup that costs nothing to host.

Questions→Gate→ Small modelorClaude→ Decision log→Retrain, gated→Model release→Hot swap, or roll back
Tests and a quality gateEvery change runs 64 tests and replays a real run from cached answers. It fails if accuracy, new-topic accuracy or call savings regress.
A live serviceA small API on a free host: small model first, cached answers next, a live Claude call only on a miss, under a daily spend cap and per-visitor limits.
Monitoring that raises its handRolling escalation rate, an estimate of leaked wrong answers against a budget, per-topic stress and spend. A schedule opens a GitHub issue if something is off.
Safe model updatesA weekly retrain must pass the same thresholds before it is published. The service swaps to it, and rolls back on its own if audited disagreement rises 50% in its first 500 questions.
State that survives restartsThe log, the spend and the model version are stored off the host, so a restart on a free tier loses nothing.
Traffic without a personA scheduled job replays the question stream into the service every ten minutes, so it always has something to watch and learn from.
